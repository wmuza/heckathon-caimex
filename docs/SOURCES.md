# Sources

Everything Mukoti's rules and pitch rest on, with where it was read. Checked on 2026-11-25.
"PDF" means the document itself was downloaded and read. "Abstract" means only the abstract or
the official data record was read. Nothing here has been reviewed by a clinician.

## How to describe Mukoti, in the words of these sources

- Mukoti is a **quick check at reception**, not a triage system. WHO's guide for essential
  practice says the person at reception "should assess the general condition of the
  careseeker(s) immediately on arrival" and "periodically repeat this procedure if the line is
  long" (source 4, section B2). In a clinic with two nurses nobody is free to do that. Mukoti is.
- **The nurse triages.** The triage tool's own FAQ says "Non health workers such as security
  guards should not be used to perform triage" and "A triage category is not a diagnosis"
  (source 1, FAQ 3 and 14). Mukoti orders the queue and drafts a note; the level is a draft until
  a nurse accepts it.
- WHO does **not** recommend running the full triage tool on every patient at a busy health post;
  it says a "simple screening tool" is "more appropriate to outpatient settings" (source 1,
  FAQ 18). Mukoti is a screening step, which is what that advice asks for.

## Triage and danger-sign tools

1. **Interagency Integrated Triage Tool** (age 12 and over; age under 12; reference card; FAQ).
   World Health Organization, International Committee of the Red Cross, Médecins Sans Frontières.
   Cited by WHO's Bulletin as Geneva: WHO; 2023. Licence: CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO. PDF.
   https://www.who.int/tools/triage
   Used for: which signs are red (our "emergency") and yellow (our "urgent"). Examples we rely on:
   red: unresponsive; respiratory distress; heavy bleeding; active convulsions; "any two of:
   altered mental status, stiff neck, hypothermia or fever, headache"; poisoning; snake bite;
   "acute chest or abdominal pain (>50 years old)"; pregnant with heavy bleeding, severe abdominal
   pain, seizures, severe headache or visual changes; under 12: age under 2 months with fever.
   yellow: unable to feed or drink; vomits everything; recent fainting; altered mental status;
   severe pain; other trauma or burns; sexual assault; infant 8 days to 6 months old.
   Where Mukoti departs from it: the tool's red rule is "any two of" altered mental status, stiff
   neck, fever and headache. Read literally, every patient with a fever and a headache is an
   emergency, which in a malaria season is much of the queue; the first version of the note prompt
   did exactly that to an ordinary headache and cough. Mukoti asks for a stiff neck or confusion as
   one of the two, and puts a severe headache with a high fever at urgent. This narrowing is ours,
   and it is the first thing a clinician should review.
   Note: the FAQ asks that the tool's wording is not changed, and the licence is non-commercial
   and share-alike. Mukoti does not reproduce the tool. Its rules are our own patterns for signs
   the tool names. A commercial product would need WHO's permission or its own clinical list.

2. **Integrated Management of Childhood Illness: Chart Booklet.** WHO, March 2014.
   ISBN 978 92 4 150682 3. All rights reserved. PDF.
   https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241506823
   Used for: the general danger signs for a child aged 2 months to 5 years: not able to drink or
   breastfeed; vomits everything; has had convulsions; lethargic or unconscious; convulsing now.
   "A child with any general danger sign needs URGENT attention." Mukoti treats these as
   emergency, which is stricter than source 1 (yellow) for the first two.
   Zimbabwe's own IMNCI chart booklet (October 2016, filed under Zimbabwe on WHO's policy
   platform) carries the same five signs.

3. **Emergency Triage Assessment and Treatment (ETAT): Manual for participants.** WHO, 2005.
   ISBN 92 4 154687 5. All rights reserved. PDF. Updated guideline: WHO, 2016,
   ISBN 9789241510219.
   Used for: the priority sign "Tiny baby (any sick child aged under two months)", and for two
   sentences worth quoting: "Children are known to have died of a treatable condition when waiting
   in the queue", and triage of a child without emergency signs "takes on average 20 seconds".

4. **Pregnancy, Childbirth, Postpartum and Newborn Care: A guide for essential practice**, 3rd
   edition. WHO, 2015. All rights reserved. PDF.
   https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/249580
   Used for: danger signs in pregnancy that mean "go to the hospital/health centre immediately"
   (section C15): vaginal bleeding; convulsions; severe headaches with blurred vision; fever and
   too weak to get out of bed; severe abdominal pain; fast or difficult breathing. And for the
   reception "Quick check" quoted above (section B2).

5. **The South African Triage Scale (SATS): Training manual 2012.** South African Triage Group,
   Emergency Medicine Society of South Africa. Licence: CC BY-NC-SA 3.0. PDF.
   Not used by Mukoti: it needs measured vital signs. Named because judges may ask about it.

Signs in Mukoti's rules with **no published tool cited** (marked "own" in the code): thoughts of
self-harm; blood in stool, urine, vomit or cough. They are there out of caution.

## Zimbabwe: is there an official triage tool for clinics?

None was found for adults at primary clinics. EDLIZ 6th edition (2011) does not contain the word
"triage" and refers child care to IMCI. A 2025 study of three major Harare referral hospitals
reported "the absence of emergency-trained staff, lack of a formal triage system, and equipment
deficiencies" (Deka P, Hodkinson P. Afr J Emerg Med 2025;15(4):100915; abstract). Whether a newer
EDLIZ has a triage chapter could not be checked.

## What not to claim

- That triage is proven to save lives. WHO's 2025 review says the evidence "may have been subject
  to confounding and publication bias" (Mitchell R et al., Bull World Health Organ
  2025;103:204–212).
- That Mukoti is clinically validated. It is not.
- Any figure for patients per nurse at Zimbabwean clinics. None was found.
- That thousands of nurses left "since 2019": seen only in news reports, no primary document.

## Data used by the prediction pages

- **Province boundaries of Zimbabwe.** geoBoundaries, gbOpen ZWE ADM1 (2020), from the Zimbabwe
  National Statistics Agency (ZIMSTAT) and OCHA ROSEA. Licence: CC BY 3.0 IGO.
  https://www.geoboundaries.org . Downloaded 2026-11-26 and simplified for display in
  `web/zw_provinces.json`.
- **Weather.** Open-Meteo forecast API, daily `precipitation_sum` and `temperature_2m_mean` at
  each province's centre point, 56 days back and 7 days ahead. Licence: CC BY 4.0.
  https://open-meteo.com . A copy fetched on 2026-11-26 is in `samples/weather_snapshot.json`.
- **Everything else on those pages is made up** and labelled so: ward names and shapes, visit
  history, water-point reports, provincial case counts, the reported outbreak, medicine stock.
- **The reasons behind the national score** (rain and unsafe water favour diarrhoeal disease;
  rain some weeks earlier and warmth favour malaria; cold favours chest infections) are stated
  from general public-health knowledge. No source was checked for them and no weight was fitted.
